Bots

Bots on PassGram.

Everything about bots: how to make one, how to run it, what it can do, how verification works, and the rules. Every title has its own link (#). The full method list is in the Bot API reference.

What is a bot#

A bot is a PassGram account run by a program instead of a person. People chat with it like with anyone else; your program reads their messages and answers through the Bot API. Bots can answer questions, send files and photos, show buttons, run shops, support desks, alerts and games.

Bots are marked with bot under their name, and they can only write to people who opened them and pressed Start.

Create your bot#

  1. In the PassGram app, open Zbot: tap the link, it opens the chat in the app.
  2. Send /newbot, then the bot's name, then a username ending as you like (4–32 letters, digits or _).
  3. Zbot shows the bot's token once. Copy it and keep it secret.
  4. Write your bot with the SDK and run it.

Talk to your bot#

Open your bot (search its username or open zpas.org/yourbot) and press Start. Your program receives that as /start. Everything people send arrives as updates; everything your bot sends shows up instantly in their app.

How bots work, step by step#

Before writing code it helps to see the whole picture. A PassGram bot is made of three parts that talk to each other: the PassGram app on a person's phone or computer, the PassGram servers, and your program, which can run anywhere: your laptop, a small server, or a cloud function.

Your program never talks to the person's app directly, and the app never talks to your program directly. Everything goes through PassGram. That is what keeps people's accounts safe: your bot never learns their phone number, email address or password, and you never have to open a port on your home computer.

Here is the full journey of one message, from the moment someone types it to the moment they see your answer:

  1. The person writes. In the chat with your bot they type /start and press send. The app sends it to PassGram, signed with their account.
  2. PassGram stores it as an update. The message becomes an Update with a number (update_id) and waits in your bot's queue. It waits up to 24 hours.
  3. Your program receives it. Either your program asks "anything new?" (long polling), or PassGram pushes the update to your server the moment it arrives (webhook).
  4. Your code decides what to do. With the SDK, the right handler runs: @bot.command("start") for commands, @bot.button(...) for button presses, @bot.message() for everything else.
  5. Your program answers. It calls a method such as sendMessage with the chat ID and the text. PassGram checks your token, checks that the person started your bot, and stores the message.
  6. The person sees it. The app shows your answer instantly, with your buttons, formatting and pictures.

The whole round trip usually takes well under a second. When the person presses one of your buttons, the same journey happens again, only the update is a CallbackQuery instead of a message.

Two consequences are worth remembering. First, your bot only works while your program runs: if you close it, messages wait in the queue and are delivered when it starts again (within 24 hours). Second, PassGram never runs your code; it only passes messages. That keeps you free to use any language, any server and any database.

Your token in depth#

The token is the only thing that proves "this program is this bot". It looks like 8513445453:GQk3yXn2…. The part before the colon is your bot's public ID; the 43 characters after it are a secret.

PassGram stores only a fingerprint (a hash) of the token, never the token itself. That is why Zbot shows it only once, when the bot is created. If you lose it, nobody can show it to you again, not even PassGram: you create a new one with /revoke, and the old one stops working at that very moment.

Where to keep it. Treat it exactly like a password:

  • Keep it in an environment variable (PASSGRAM_BOT_TOKEN) or a .env file that is never shared.
  • Never put it in a GitHub repository, a screenshot, a chat, a web page or a mobile app. Code that runs on people's devices can always be read.
  • If you share your code, share it without the token, and add .env to your .gitignore.
  • If you even suspect it leaked, send /revoke to Zbot. It takes ten seconds and makes the old token worthless.

What a leaked token allows. Someone with your token can read the messages people send to your bot and answer as your bot. They cannot log in to your PassGram account, see your other chats or your vault: the token only controls the bot.

Polling or webhook: which one to choose#

Your program can receive updates in two ways. Both deliver exactly the same Update objects; the difference is who starts the conversation.

Long polling means your program asks PassGram "is there anything new for me?" and PassGram keeps the question open for up to 25 seconds until something arrives. As soon as a message comes in, the answer returns, your program handles it and asks again. This is what bot.run() does. Polling is perfect while you develop and for bots that run on your own computer, because it needs no public address, no certificate and no open port. It works behind any home router or company firewall.

A webhook turns it around: you give PassGram an https:// address, and PassGram sends every update to it the moment it happens. Nothing runs while nobody writes to your bot, which makes webhooks ideal for servers and for cloud functions that only wake up when called. The price is that you need a public https address.

Long pollingWebhook
Needs a public addressNoYes (https)
Works on a home PCYesOnly with a tunnel
DelayUnder a secondInstant

Best forDevelopment, small botsProduction, serverless

Delivery is "at least once". If your server crashes in the middle of handling an update, or answers with an error, PassGram sends that update again. Very rarely you might therefore see the same update twice. If an action must never happen twice (charging money, creating an order), remember the update_id or your own order ID and skip duplicates.

Order is kept. Updates for your bot are delivered in the order they happened. When a webhook delivery fails, later updates wait behind it, so a person's messages never arrive in the wrong order.

Designing a good bot#

The best bots feel like a helpful person, not like a form. These ten rules make the biggest difference:

  1. Say what you do in the first message. When someone presses Start, tell them in one or two sentences what your bot does and show the two or three most important buttons. Nobody reads long welcome texts.
  2. Always answer. Every message deserves a reaction, even if it is "I didn't understand that, here is what I can do". Silence makes people think the bot is broken.
  3. Answer fast, or show that you are working. If something takes longer than a second, call sendChatAction so the person sees "typing…".
  4. Prefer buttons over typing. Tapping is faster and leaves no room for typos. Use free text only where the answer is truly free (a name, a question).
  5. Edit instead of spamming. When the person moves through a menu, edit the same message (editMessageText) instead of sending a new one each time.
  6. Give a way back. Every screen of a menu should have a "Back" or "Main menu" button, and every multi-step flow should accept /cancel.
  7. Remember context, but not forever. Keep the state of a conversation (which step someone is on) and clear it when the task is finished.
  8. Speak the person's language. Use language_code to answer in their language when you can.
  9. Be honest about what you are. Never pretend to be a person, a PassGram employee or another company.
  10. Fail gracefully. When something goes wrong, apologise briefly, say what the person can do, and log the details for yourself.

Writing messages people read#

Messages in a chat are read on a small screen, often in a hurry. Short, clear texts work best:

  • One idea per message. If you have three things to say, consider three short messages or one message with three short lines.
  • Put the important part first. "✅ Order #1042 is on its way" beats "We wanted to let you know that your order…".
  • Use formatting sparingly. Bold for the one thing that matters, code for things people copy (codes, IDs). A message where everything is bold has no emphasis left.
  • Emoji are signposts, not decoration. One emoji at the start of a line (📦, ✅, ⚠️) helps people scan; five in a row slows them down.
  • Write buttons as actions. "Pay $4.50" and "Show my orders" are clearer than "OK" and "Next".
  • Escape user text. When you include something people typed (their name, their message) in a formatted message, escape it, as shown in the formatting example.

Choosing good commands#

Commands are the shortcuts people see when they tap Menu or type /. A good command list is short and predictable.

  • /start is sent automatically when someone opens your bot for the first time. Make it useful every time, not only the first time: many people send it again to "reset" the bot.
  • /help should explain in a few lines what the bot does and list the main commands.
  • Keep the list between three and seven commands. Rarely used features belong behind buttons.
  • Use lowercase English words without spaces (/orders, /settings); the description next to each command can be in any language.
  • Set the list with setMyCommands when your bot starts, so it is always in sync with your code.

Choosing the right buttons#

PassGram bots have three kinds of buttons. Choosing the right one makes a bot feel natural.

Inline buttons sit under a message and belong to it. Use them for choices about that message: "Buy", "Next page", "Yes / No", "Show details". A press sends your bot a CallbackQuery with the button's value, but does not post anything in the chat. That keeps the conversation clean. Up to 30 buttons per message, in rows of up to 4.

Reply keyboards replace the phone keyboard with big buttons. A tap sends the label as a normal message from the person, visible in the chat. Use them for quick answers to a question you just asked: sizes, ratings, yes/no. Add one_time_keyboard so it disappears after the answer, and remove it with remove_keyboard when you no longer need it.

The menu button next to the message box opens your command list, or a mini app (your own web page inside the chat).

Designing button values. The value of an inline button (callback_data) can hold up to 64 bytes. Use a short, structured format such as order:1042 or page:3, so one handler can read what was pressed. Never put secrets or prices you trust in button values: treat them like any input that comes from the person.

Remembering things and handling data#

Many bots need to remember something: which step of a form someone is on, their settings, their orders. Where you keep that information matters.

In memory (a dictionary or a Map in your program) is the simplest and is fine for short conversations. Everything is lost when the program restarts, so use it only for things that are cheap to lose, like the step of a form.

In a file or a database for everything that must survive a restart. SQLite needs no server and is built into Python; for bigger bots, PostgreSQL or a hosted database. The notes example shows both a database and a simple file.

Keep only what you need. The less you store, the less can leak. A bot that sends reminders needs the chat ID and the reminder text, not the person's name or anything else.

Let people delete their data. Offer a command such as /deletemydata that removes everything you stored about the person, and do it for real. In many countries this is also the law.

Say what you store. If your bot keeps data, explain in /help or in your description what you keep and why.

Security checklist for bot builders#

  • The token lives in an environment variable or a private .env file, never in code you share.
  • Your repository's .gitignore lists .env and any database files.
  • Webhook servers check the X-ZPass-Bot-Api-Secret-Token header on every request.
  • All text from people is treated as untrusted: escaped before it goes into formatted messages, validated before it goes into a database, never executed.
  • Database queries use parameters (? placeholders), never text glued together.
  • Button values are checked: a person can send any value they like, so never trust a price or an ID just because it came from your own button.
  • Admin commands check the sender's ID against a list you control.
  • Your bot never asks for passwords, recovery keys, one-time codes or card numbers.
  • Error messages to people are friendly and short; technical details go only to your own logs.
  • Logs do not contain tokens or private message contents you do not need.
  • Dependencies (if you use any) are kept up to date.
  • You know how to /revoke the token, and you do it at once if it may have leaked.

Testing your bot#

Test like a stranger would use your bot, not like the person who wrote it:

  1. Start fresh. Clear the chat with your bot and press Start again. Is the first message clear?
  2. Try the happy path. Go through each feature the normal way.
  3. Try the unhappy paths. Send text where the bot expects a button, press old buttons from earlier messages, send /cancel in the middle of a form, send an empty-looking message, send something very long.
  4. Restart the bot in the middle. Stop your program while a form is half filled in, start it again, and see what happens.
  5. Use a second account. Ask a friend, or use a second account, to check that one person's data never appears for another.
  6. Watch your logs. Every error printed while testing is one your users would have hit.

The SDK keeps running when a handler fails, so one bug never stops the whole bot, but it still deserves a fix.

Going-live checklist#

  • Name, description (/setdescription), short description (/setabouttext) and profile picture are set.
  • The command list is set and matches what the bot really does.
  • /start and /help explain the bot in a few lines.
  • The bot runs as a service that restarts after crashes and reboots (see keeping your bot online).
  • For production, consider a webhook instead of polling.
  • The token is stored safely and nobody else has it.
  • You tested the unhappy paths above.
  • If the bot stores data, /help says what and why, and people can delete it.
  • You read the rules for bots. If your bot represents a company or a public figure, apply for the blue check.

Speed, limits and big audiences#

Each bot can make 120 requests per minute. That is plenty for answering people one by one, because every answer is one request. It starts to matter when your bot writes to many people at once, for example an announcement.

Do the math. 1,000 people at 100 messages per minute takes 10 minutes. Send in a loop with a short pause (about 0.6 seconds) between messages, as in the announcements example, and you will never hit the limit.

If you get 429. The answer contains retry_after: wait that many seconds, then continue. Never retry in a tight loop; that only extends the wait.

Keep handlers fast. With polling, the SDK handles updates one after another. If one handler waits 30 seconds for another service, everyone else waits too. For slow work, answer first ("Working on it…"), then do the work in the background (a thread in Python, a promise you don't await in JavaScript), or use a webhook server that handles requests in parallel.

Files. Sending a link to a file on your server costs PassGram nothing and has no size limit; uploading goes through PassGram and is limited to 20 MB. For big or popular files, links are faster for everyone.

What bots can do#

  • Text with formatting: bold, italic, code, links, spoilers.
  • Inline buttons under messages, and reply keyboards above the message box.
  • Photos, videos, GIFs, audio, voice, files.
  • Edit and delete their messages, show typing….

Files and media#

Send a link (https://…) to a file on your own server: any size, PassGram stores nothing. Or upload a file up to 20 MB: PassGram only passes it on, keeps it until the person downloads it once (at most 24 hours), then deletes it.

Links to your bot#

Share zpas.org/yourbot: it opens the chat with your bot in the app. PassGram's own bots: zpas.org/zbot and zpas.org/verifybot.

Verify Bot#

Verify Bot (zpas.org/verifybot) is PassGram's official bot for blue checks. It only does verification.

CommandWhat it does
/start, /verifyApply for your account or one of your bots.
/myverifyStatus of your requests (waiting, approved, or rejected with the reason).
/unverifyRemove a blue check from your account or bot.

How to apply:

  1. Choose what to verify: your account or one of your bots (you can only apply for things you own).
  2. Send links to at least 2 official accounts on well-known platforms: Instagram, X, YouTube, TikTok, Facebook, your official website or a Wikipedia page.
  3. Add your Z Pass link (zpas.org/yourname) to the bio of those accounts, so we can see they belong to you.
  4. Optional: up to 2 press articles that mention you, a company name, and a short note.
  5. The PassGram team reviews it. Approved checks appear automatically.

The blue check#

A blue check means the account or bot is the official one of that person, brand or organization, and PassGram confirmed it. It is not an endorsement of what they say, and it gives no extra features. It appears next to the name in chats, the chat list, search and the profile.

New bots are never verified automatically; only PassGram can grant a check.

Company names#

Verified organizations can also show a company name next to their name, e.g. Z Pass Inc. You can suggest one when you apply; PassGram decides what is shown (up to 32 characters).

Remove a check#

A verified name or username should not change. To rename, send /unverify to Verify Bot first, make the change, then apply again.

Rules for bots#

  • Never ask people for passwords, recovery keys, one-time codes or card numbers. PassGram never does.
  • No spam, scams, impersonation, malware or illegal content.
  • Only message people who started your bot, and stop when they ask.
  • Say clearly what your bot does and who runs it.
  • Keep your token safe; you are responsible for what your bot does.

Full rules: Terms.

Privacy#

Private chats between people are end-to-end encrypted; bots cannot see them. Chats with a bot are different: the bot's owner reads them (the bot must, to answer) and they pass through the PassGram server. Bots only learn a person's Z Pass ID, name, username and app language; never their phone, email, password or vault.

Reports and bans#

People can report bots. PassGram sees the bot's owner and can disable or delete bots that break the rules. A disabled bot's API calls return 403.

Tutorial: your first bot in 5 minutes#

  1. In the app, open Zbot, send /newbot, choose a name and a username. Copy the token.
  2. Download passgram.py into a new folder.
  3. Next to it create bot.py:
from passgram import Bot

bot = Bot("PASTE_YOUR_TOKEN_HERE")

@bot.command("start")
def start(msg):
    msg.reply(f"Hello {msg.user.first_name}! Send me anything and I will repeat it.")

@bot.message()
def echo(msg):
    msg.reply("You said: " + msg.text)

bot.run()
const { Bot } = require('./passgram');

const bot = new Bot('PASTE_YOUR_TOKEN_HERE');
bot.command('start', (msg) => msg.reply(`Hello ${msg.user.firstName}! Send me anything and I will repeat it.`));
bot.message((msg) => msg.reply('You said: ' + msg.text));
bot.run();
  1. Run it: python bot.py (or node bot.js). You will see PassGram bot @yourbot is running.
  2. Open your bot in the app and press Start. That's it: your bot answers.

bot = Bot("TOKEN")
bot.set_commands({"start": "Main menu", "help": "How it works"})

    msg.reply("What would you like?", buttons=MENU)

def size(msg):
    msg.reply(f"{msg.text} it is! ✅", keyboard=Keyboard.remove())

bot.run()

Tutorial: a small shop bot#

A shop shows products with photos, takes the order with buttons and confirms it. Payment happens on your own website (see bots and money).

from passgram import Bot, Buttons

bot = Bot("TOKEN")
PRODUCTS = {"latte": ("Latte", 4.50, "https://cdn.example.com/latte.jpg"),
            "mocha": ("Mocha", 5.00, "https://cdn.example.com/mocha.jpg")}

@bot.command("start")
def start(msg):
    for key, (name, price, photo) in PRODUCTS.items():
        bot.send_photo(msg.chat_id, photo, caption=f"{name} — ${price:.2f}",
                       buttons=Buttons([("🛒 Order", f"order:{key}")]))

@bot.button()                      # every press comes here
def order(press):
    if not press.data.startswith("order:"):
        return
    name, price, _ = PRODUCTS[press.data.split(":")[1]]
    press.answer("Added ✅")
    press.reply(f"<b>{name}</b> ordered. Pay here:", format="html",
                buttons=Buttons([("💳 Pay $%.2f" % price, "https://shop.example.com/pay?item=" + name)]))

bot.run()

Tutorial: move an existing bot#

  1. Create a bot in Zbot and copy the new token.
  2. In your code, replace the old token and set the server address to https://api.zpas.org (see the setting for your library).
  3. Run it. Text, buttons, keyboards, photos, files, edits and webhooks work the same.
  4. Check what bots can do: features like groups or payments are not available yet.

FAQ#

Does my bot work when my PC is off?

No. Your bot is your program; it runs where you start it. Run it on a server for 24/7. Messages sent while it is off wait up to 24 hours.

I lost my token.

Send /revoke to Zbot. You get a new token and the old one stops working.

Why does my bot get 403?

The person has not pressed Start in your bot yet, or the bot was disabled.

Can I use code written for another platform?

Usually yes. See existing libraries.

How do I get the blue check?

Apply with Verify Bot.

Is the Bot API free?
